# Frameworks: foundations, value creation, concession strategy

Each entry: what it is, when to use, one invented example line. All names, figures and companies are invented; check any rule-of-thumb number against your own market.

## Group 1. Foundations

**1. BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement)**
What: the best outcome you get if this deal fails, valued in today's terms. It sets your real power, not your wishes.
Use: before anything else; strengthen it before you negotiate if it is weak.
Example: "My freelance retainer elsewhere would bring 2,400 a month by next month, so I will not accept less than that net of extra effort."

**2. Reservation point (walk-away)**
What: the worst deal you would still accept, set from your BATNA plus the cost of switching, written down before talks.
Use: always; never reveal it, and never drift past it under pressure.
Example: "Below 3,100 all-in, or without the delivery clause, I walk."

**3. ZOPA (zone of possible agreement)**
What: the overlap between your reservation point and theirs. If none exists, no deal is possible without changing the terms or the alternatives.
Use: to judge whether a deal is feasible and where the likely landing zone is.
Example: "They can pay up to about 90 and I need at least 75, so the zone is 75 to 90; I open near 98."

**4. Interests vs positions**
What: a position is what someone demands; an interest is why. Several positions can satisfy one interest.
Use: when talks stall on a figure or a clause; ask why it matters.
Example: "You insist on 30-day payment; is the real concern cash flow or approval cycles?"

**5. Counterparty map (four lenses)**
What: for the other side, write their interests, constraints (budget, rules, approvals), alternatives, and how the situation looks from their chair.
Use: in preparation and after every surprise.
Example: "Their buyer needs sign-off from finance by month end, so a quarter-close discount matters to them."

## Group 2. Value creation

**6. Fixed-pie check and issue expansion**
What: most negotiators assume one issue (price) and a fixed total. Check for other issues (timing, scope, risk, volume, support, brand) that could grow the total or be traded.
Use: whenever the talk is a price tug-of-war.
Example: "Beyond the fee, can we discuss start date, payment timing and a referral clause?"

**7. Cross-priority trade (logrolling)**
What: give the other side what they value more than you do, in return for what you value more than they do.
Use: when two or more issues are on the table and the sides rank them differently.
Example: "I will take the longer payment term you want if you commit to the larger first order."

**8. Post-deal review**
What: after a deal is reached, invite a short second pass to find terms both sides prefer, with the agreed deal as the floor for each.
Use: for complex or long-term deals where trust is valuable.
Example: "We have a deal; before we sign, can each of us name one change that would leave us both better off?"

## Group 3. Concession strategy

**9. Concession pacing**
What: concede in decreasing steps, always conditionally ("if you do X, I can do Y"), and never alone: each move should invite one in return. Do not concede on a deadline you invented.
Use: any multi-round negotiation.
Example: "Moving from 120 to 114, then to 111, shows I am near my limit."

**10. Shrinking-step counter sequence**
What: plan a series of offers whose gaps get smaller each time, ending on an odd, precise figure and a small non-money extra. It signals that you have little room left.
Use: price haggling where you must respond to a number you reject.
Reference point: rounds at roughly 65, 85, 95 and 100 percent of your target, adjusted to the case; test against the market.
Example: "My final number is 97,400, and I would add the training days as a goodwill item."

**11. Deadline handling**
What: most concessions land close to a deadline. Know whose deadline it is, set a real one if you have one, and never reveal that yours is tight.
Use: when time pressure appears on either side.
Example: "I can decide by Friday because a competing quote expires then; after that the terms change."
