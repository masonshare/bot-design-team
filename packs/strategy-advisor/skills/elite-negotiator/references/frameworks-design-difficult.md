# Frameworks: deal design, sequencing, difficult counterparties, ethics

Each entry: what it is, when to use, one invented example line. Invented examples; check any rule-of-thumb figure against your own market.

## Group 5. Deal design and sequencing

**23. First offer or second offer**
What: going first usually sets the anchor, if you know the market well enough to avoid underbidding. If you know little, let them speak first and listen for information.
Use: deciding who opens.
Example: "I will let you share your budget first; I want to be sure my proposal fits."

**24. Perspective-taking**
What: understand how they think and what they need (a mental exercise), without necessarily sharing their feelings. It finds more options than sympathy does.
Use: always, especially when they seem irrational.
Example: "From their side the risk is the internal blame for a missed launch, so I will offer a phased start."

**25. Three-layer setup**
What: negotiate on three layers: setup (right people, right sequence, right alternatives before any talk), design (the structure of the deal), tactics (the conversation). Most wins and losses come from the first two.
Use: complex deals; run through it in preparation.
Example: "Before pricing, I need the finance director in the room and a written scope."

**26. Sequencing and coalitions**
What: the order in which you approach people changes the outcome. Start with the supporter who has influence, build agreed points, then approach the doubters; map who must say yes, who can block, and who influences them.
Use: multi-party or multi-stakeholder deals.
Example: "I will brief operations first; their support makes the budget holder's decision easier."

**27. Information exchange**
What: trade information deliberately: share your interests and priorities (not your limit) in return for theirs. Ask before you reveal, and offer in steps so the exchange stays balanced.
Use: to find trades; avoid when the other side only extracts.
Example: "Timeline matters more to us than price; what is most important on your side?"

## Group 6. Difficult counterparties

**28. Weak-position moves**
What: with a poor alternative, shift power by: reducing dependence (improve your alternative), widening the issues, offering a distinct value they cannot get elsewhere, building allies, and being very prepared on facts.
Use: when they hold most cards.
Example: "I cannot match the discount, but I can offer the integration support the other vendor does not provide."

**29. Deadlock levers**
What: three levers for a stalled talk: reframe (change how the problem is stated), change the process (new forum, smaller group, pause, expert), and address the emotional block (acknowledge what is unsaid).
Use: when both sides have repeated positions without movement.
Example: "Let us stop debating the figure and list what each side would need for it to be a fair outcome."

**30. Five-step de-escalation**
What: (1) pause and calm yourself before responding, (2) listen and acknowledge their view, (3) reframe their position as a joint problem, (4) make it easy for them to say yes, with a face-saving path and a way for them to explain it internally, (5) if still stuck, show consequences of no deal factually, without threats.
Use: hostile, stonewalling or take-it-or-leave-it counterparties.
Example: "I understand why this is firm for you. How could we describe an arrangement that your board accepts and my team can deliver?"

## Ethics line (check before each tactic)

Fair play: preparation, honesty about what you say, strong anchors with reasons, real alternatives, real deadlines, silence, questions, showing consequences.
Not allowed: made-up offers or deadlines, false facts, bait and switch, personal attacks, unlawful threats, exploiting confidential information or someone's vulnerability.
Test: "Could I say this tactic aloud to them without it falling apart?" If not, drop it.
Legal wording, enforceability, employment or securities questions: flag for a qualified professional. This library is strategy advice, not legal advice.
