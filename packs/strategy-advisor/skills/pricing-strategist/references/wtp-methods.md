# Willingness-to-pay methods: compact guide

Use only when the user wants to measure willingness to pay (deep mode, or when the memo's assumptions rest on it).

## 1. Buyer interviews (5 to 10, 20 minutes each)
Goal: learn the job, the current spend and the cost of failure, not a price quote.
Questions, in order:
1. Tell me about the last time you needed to [job]. What did you do?
2. What did that cost you in money, time and effort?
3. What went wrong or was annoying? What did that cost?
4. What have you tried and dropped? Why?
5. If this problem vanished, what would it be worth to you or your business?
Avoid: "would you pay X?" (people say yes to be polite). After the interviews, summarise the spend ranges per segment and mark them as qualitative.

## 2. Four-question price-sensitivity survey
Describe the product in 3 to 5 lines, then ask each respondent, in randomised order:
- At what price is it so cheap you would doubt its quality?
- At what price is it a bargain?
- At what price does it start to feel expensive, but you would still consider it?
- At what price is it too expensive to consider?
Analysis: plot the cumulative share for each question across prices. The crossing of "too cheap" and "expensive" marks the low edge of the acceptable range; the crossing of "bargain" and "too expensive" marks the high edge. The crossing of "too cheap" and "too expensive" is a point of least resistance, not automatically the best price. Reference points for sample size: about 100 or more per segment for consumer goods, fewer for narrow business buyers; check against your audience. Segment the results. Pair with the value estimate from the interviews.

## 3. Step-by-step accept/reject
Show one price at a time (random starting point, rising or falling), ask "would you buy at this price?" Plot the share saying yes at each price to get a rough demand curve and an elasticity estimate. Stated intent usually overstates real buying, so scale results down or confirm with a live test.

## 4. Choice tasks (conjoint style)
Show two or three bundles with different features and prices and ask which the respondent would pick. Useful when deciding which features belong in which tier. Needs a tool or a statistician; only worth it for a large decision.

## 5. Live experiments
- Split new visitors at random between prices; compare revenue per visitor.
- Or run price periods one after another with the same promotion load, accepting weaker evidence.
- Or limit a new price to one channel or region first.
Always record: traffic source, promotion load, stock availability, seasonality, so a result is not credited to price when something else changed.

## 6. Reading results
- A range from a survey is a starting hypothesis, not an answer.
- If measured willingness is far below your value estimate, the issue is usually positioning, trust or the wrong job, not the price itself. Fix that before cutting price.
- Record the date: willingness to pay shifts with competitors, costs and mood. Re-check each quarter in fast markets.
