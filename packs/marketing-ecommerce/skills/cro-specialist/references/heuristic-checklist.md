# Heuristic checklist (compact)

Use during a diagnosis. Walk every line of the chosen block, mark pass / weak / fail, and attach evidence (a screenshot note, a metric, a quote from a user). All thresholds here are reference points, not facts about your market.

## 1. LIFT, six factors on one page

| Factor | Ask | Typical fail signs |
|---|---|---|
| Value proposition | In five seconds, can a stranger say what is offered, for whom, and why it beats the alternative? | Generic headline, features without outcomes, all tiers or options look equal |
| Relevance | Does the page match what the visitor expected when they clicked? | Ad says one thing and the page another, wrong audience language |
| Clarity | Is there one obvious main action and a readable hierarchy? | Dense blocks, jargon, several competing buttons |
| Urgency | Is there a genuine reason to act now? | No deadline, no cost of waiting. Only use real urgency, never invented scarcity |
| Anxiety | What might the visitor fear (price, commitment, privacy, quality) and is it answered where the fear arises? | Proof far from the button, hidden fees, vague guarantee |
| Distraction | Does anything pull attention off the goal? | Extra navigation, autoplay media, secondary offers beside the main one |

Value proposition read as a balance: perceived benefit and difference from rivals on one side, cost, effort and risk on the other. Points of difference matter most, points of parity are table stakes, points of irrelevance are noise to remove.

## 2. Conversion trinity (quick 5-minute check)

1. **Relevance:** right visitor, right message, right moment.
2. **Value:** is the reason to act stated in the visitor's terms?
3. **Call to action:** is the next step visible, specific and low in perceived risk?
If any one fails, momentum breaks and the visitor leaves. Run this on each step of a flow.

Add two small tests:
- **Message match:** headline, imagery and offer echo the source (ad, email, search result).
- **Visitor-focus test:** count "we" statements against "you" statements; heavy "we" copy is company-centred.
- **Trunk test:** on any page, can a new visitor tell the site, the page, the main sections, where they are and what to do next?

## 3. Clarity before persuasion

Do not test persuasion devices (scarcity, proof, authority) until the offer is understood. Order of work: clarity, then value, then friction and anxiety, then persuasion.

## 4. Friction and anxiety

- **Length versus difficulty:** a long but easy path can beat a short but confusing one.
- **Real versus perceived friction:** a five-field form that looks cluttered can lose to a seven-field form that looks calm. Check grouping, whitespace, labels, progress indicators.
- **Anxiety rule:** the reassurance must be specific, close to the worry and strong enough. A badge in the footer does not calm a worry at the card field.
- Each step asks for a small yes. Ask what the visitor gains from this yes; where cost exceeds perceived gain, they exit.

## 5. Behavioural lenses (B=MAP)

| Gap | Question | Fix direction |
|---|---|---|
| Motivation | Is the desire strong enough: pleasure or pain relief, hope or fear, belonging or rejection? | Sharper value, proof, relevant incentives |
| Ability | Is it easy in time, money, effort, thinking and routine? | Cut steps and fields, defaults, clearer wording |
| Prompt | Is there a clear trigger at the right moment? | Match the prompt to the state: a facilitator when motivated but blocked, a spark when able but unmotivated, a signal when both are fine |

Slide metaphor: add pull (value, benefit), smooth the surface (friction), and angle the page toward the goal (defaults, ordering). Non-conscious motivators (proof, authority, reciprocity, liking, commitment, scarcity, unity) usually cost less than discounts.

## 6. Usability lenses worth a pass

Visibility of status, match to real-world language, user control and undo, consistency, error prevention, recognition over recall, flexibility, minimal design, helpful error messages, available help. On mobile add: tap target size, keyboard type per field, sticky action, load time.

## 7. Evidence ladder for findings (strongest first)

1. Behavioural data from your own users (analytics by segment, recordings, form analytics).
2. Direct statements from users (surveys, interviews, support questions).
3. Observed user tests with tasks.
4. Heuristic judgement by an experienced reviewer.
5. Competitor or generic best-practice lists. Treat as ideas only, never as proof.
